# Supercell inflow and the rear-flank downdraft (research, 2026-11-03)

Rules the lab's storm should obey. Values marked (≈) are standard textbook figures that were not re-checked.

## Inflow

1. **Inflow layer.** The effective inflow layer holds the parcels with CAPE ≥ 100 J/kg and CIN ≥ -250 J/kg.
   - It is usually 1-3 km deep; Peters et al. 2020 use the lowest 2.2 km.
   - The updraft draws mostly from the lowest 1-2 km, weighted toward the bottom (Thompson et al. 2007 ≈; Peters et al. 2020, JAS 77:3033).
2. **Storm-relative inflow sets updraft width, mass flux and w** (correlation > 0.85).
   - Streamwise vorticity does not set the width. Continuity gives R ~ H·V_SR/w (Peters, Nowotarski & Morrison 2019; Peters et al. 2020).
   - Later work (Peters et al. 2023) finds meso width follows streamwise vorticity, so updraft width and meso width are separate quantities.
3. **Streamwise vorticity, not inflow speed, sets low-level rotation.**
   - The correlation of 2 km net rotation with streamwise vorticity or SRH is 0.91-0.93.
   - Its correlation with SR flow is about 0 (Peters et al. 2020).
4. **The lowest 500 m matters most** (VORTEX2: Parker 2014, MWR 142:508; Coffer & Parker 2017, 2018).
   - 0-500 m SRH: 159 m²/s² for tornadic storms against 80 for non-tornadic.
   - Inflow-to-shear angle: about 90° (streamwise) for significant tornadoes, about 110° otherwise.
5. **Inflow near the storm is 0.5-1 K cooler** within 30-40 km, from anvil shading and lifting (Parker 2014).
6. **Wall-cloud air comes mostly from the forward flank** (Rotunno & Klemp 1985; Atkins, Glidden & Nicholson 2014, MWR 142:4823). It is rain-cooled, near-saturated air carried along the forward-flank boundary and lifted by the low-level updraft.
   - That air is about 4 K cooler (θv) and 1.5 g/kg drier than the inflow.
   - Its LCL is about 530 m, against an observed wall-cloud base of 560 m and a main base of 1.1-1.3 km.
   - Wall clouds hang 700-800 m below the base and are 1.6-8 km across.
   - The meso's -8 to -10 hPa adds 140-160 m of lowering.
   - In a non-tornadic case the wall cloud was 3 km from the meso, because strong outflow had displaced the updraft.

## Rear-flank downdraft

7. **Buoyancy decides tornado production** (Markowski, Straka & Rasmussen 2002, MWR 130:1692). Surface values within 2-4 km of the circulation:

   | Storm | θv deficit | θe deficit | CIN |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Non-tornadic | ≥ 5 K | about 10 K | often 200-500 J/kg |
   | Weakly tornadic | 4-7 K | 10-12 K | about 150 J/kg |
   | Prolific (F2+ or > 5 min) | about 2 K | about 4 K | small: RFD air keeps surface-based CAPE |

   - Moist, low-LCL environments make warm RFDs.
   - Rule: an RFD colder than about 3-4 K θv starves the tornado.
8. **Internal surges are driven by the pressure gradient under the low-level meso, not by buoyancy** (Skinner et al. 2014, MWR 142).
   - Significant tornadoes form when the RFD is kinematically strong but only weakly negatively buoyant (Lee, Finley & Karstens 2012, Bowdle).
   - A colder surge replacing the buoyant air coincided with decay.
   - A surge boundary's convergence reaching the circulation came before rapid strengthening.
9. **Maintenance.** A tornado lasts while it sits under the mid-level updraft, in the rear-flank gust front's convergence. It dies when the updraft tilts away or the convergence is displaced by several km (Marquis et al. 2012, MWR 140:3).
10. **Occlusion downdraft.** When rotation near the ground exceeds rotation aloft, the pressure is lowest at the ground, which forces air down at the meso centre (Klemp & Rotunno 1983, JAS 40:359). A ring of vorticity then favours multiple vortices.
11. **Cycle timing.**
    - Simulated: the first occlusion takes about 2 h, then the cycle repeats about every 60 min (Adlerman, Droegemeier & Davies-Jones 1999).
    - Observed cycles are often 20-45 min (≈).
12. **Surface pressure** in the RFD is up to a few hPa above ambient (Markowski 2002 review, MWR 130:852).
13. **Clear slot.** Dry, warmed sinking RFD air clears the base in a horseshoe that wraps at least two thirds of the way round the tornado. The cyclonic tornado spot is on its north side; anticyclonic vortices form on its south side.

Disagreements:
- **RFD origin height.** Lemon & Doswell 1979 say mid levels. The surface θe in Markowski 2002 points to 1.5-2.4 km.
- **Cold secondary RFDs.** Marquis 2012 says they can help maintenance; Markowski and Lee say they hurt.
- **Occlusion period.** About 60 min in models against shorter observed cycles.
- **Surge speeds.** Not retrieved: about 20-35 m/s near the tornado (≈, unverified).

## Visible inflow features

All inflow cloud is air on its way into the updraft base. It lies in the inflow sector or along the outer edge of the forward-flank boundary, outside the precipitation core, and ends at the updraft base or wall cloud.

- **Beaver tail (flumen).** A low, broad, flat band attached to the updraft base, roughly parallel to the forward-flank gust front.
  - It sits on the inflow-facing (southern) edge of the forward-flank rain and below the main base, not under the core.
  - It can be 16+ km long, may show Kelvin-Helmholtz billows on its shear interface, and its elements move storm-relatively toward the updraft.
  - Its base is near the LCL of the boundary-lifted, partly cooled air, between the wall cloud's base and the main base.
  - Sources: NSSL Conway 2001 case; NWS RAC glossary; NWS spotter guide.
- **Tail cloud (cauda).** A short band, about 1 km to a few km, from the wall cloud toward the rain core on the forward-flank side, at about the wall cloud's base height.
  - Its elements flow into the wall cloud, rising where they join.
  - It runs over the forward-flank outflow and stops at the rain curtain's edge; it does not go into the core.
- **Inflow bands.** Cumulus or stratocumulus lines in the inflow sector (south to east) curving into the updraft base. The stronger the meso, the stronger the curvature.
- **Scud (pannus).** Ragged fragments where rain-cooled air is lifted. Rising scud can merge into the wall cloud.
- Spotter sources mix up beaver tail and tail cloud; the WMO Cloud Atlas terms flumen and cauda are the reference.

**Placement rule (cannot intersect the storm):**
1. Seed parcels at the updraft base near the LCL.
2. Integrate them backward in the storm-relative wind for 10-20 min.
3. Draw cloud only on segments where the parcel has been lifted to its own LCL, which is lowered inside the cold pool.
4. Clip each path where it enters the storm's cloud volume, the precipitation core or the RFD (w < 0).

## Cheap formulations

- **Rotation pressure.** p' ≈ -ρV² at the meso (V 20-25 m/s gives -5 to -7 hPa).
  - Vertical force: a = -(1/ρ)∂p'/∂z. It gives lift where rotation strengthens with height, and pushes air down (the occlusion / RFD) where near-ground rotation is stronger than rotation aloft.
- **Linear shear pressure.** p'_L ∝ (∂V_h/∂z)·∇_h w: high upshear, low downshear, which favours the right flank.
- **Buoyancy.** B = g(θ'/θ + 0.61 q_v' - q_c - q_r).
  - 1 g/kg of loading is worth about 0.3 K; 1 g/kg evaporated cools by 2.5 K.
  - Downdraft speeds stay well under √(2 DCAPE).
- **RFD strength.** w_RFD = -[a_dyn(meso V, ∂V/∂z) + B(rain, evaporation)].
  - The dynamic part dominates surges.
  - The deficit grows with evaporation and with environmental dryness (high LCL).
- **Gust-front speed.** c = k √(g h Δθv/θv), k 0.7-1. For 1 km depth and 3 K, c is 7-10 m/s.
- **Wall-cloud base.** The LCL of the air feeding it (mostly forward-flank air), minus about 14 m per hPa of meso deficit.
